Slide 31:00 · 40 s

Why it matters. Nickel-rich battery cathodes are made from lithium hydroxide monohydrate. Tesla's refinery in Robstown, Texas turns ore into it, about twenty thousand tonnes a year, enough for roughly thirty gigawatt-hours of batteries. The last step is crystallization: boil water off a purified brine until crystals form. Boiling is where the energy goes, and MVR is how industry makes it cheaper. The fifty-five to sixty percent market share is a market-research estimate, so I label it as one.

Slide 41:40 · 60 s

Point along the loop as you talk: feed → crystallizer → vapor → compressor → heater → back.

Here's the loop. Ten thousand kilograms an hour of brine at six percent lithium hydroxide enters the crystallizer, which boils at sixty degrees under vacuum. The vapor goes to the compressor, which squeezes it from 16.5 to 26 kilopascals. At the higher pressure it condenses at sixty-six degrees, six degrees hotter than the liquor, so it can heat the liquor in the heater. That's the whole trick. Crystals come out the bottom, and a small purge bleeds off impurities. The punchline: boiling needs about 5,600 kilowatts of heat, but the compressor only spends about 250 kilowatts to recycle the vapor's own heat.

Plain words: "latent heat" = the energy it takes to turn water into steam; you get it back when the steam condenses. MVR re-uses it instead of burning fuel for new steam.

Slide 73:35 · 55 s

Salt makes the compressor work harder. Dissolved lithium and hydroxide ions lower water's vapor pressure, so the liquor boils about four degrees hotter than pure water at the same pressure. That's the boiling-point elevation, or BPE. The vapor that comes off is pure water, so the compressor has to lift it from water's boiling point, not the liquor's. So BPE is pure penalty: about 4 of the 10 degrees the compressor has to lift. Ideal theory says 4.76 degrees; the real electrolyte gives 3.95, because concentrated lithium hydroxide behaves as if it had fewer free particles. Ideal theory would overestimate it by about 21 percent.

If asked "how does that make sense?": BPE = φ · i · Kb · m. i = 2 ions per LiOH, m ≈ 6 mol/kg (saturated), Kb = 0.39 at 60 °C (smaller than the textbook 0.512 at 100 °C because Kb scales with T²/hfg), φ = 0.83 is the osmotic coefficient.

Slide 94:35 · 55 s

Lesson two: MVR only pays for the lift. The compressor doesn't supply the heat to boil water; that heat is already in the vapor. It only raises the vapor's condensing temperature by about ten degrees, from 56 to 66. So 99 kilojoules of work moves 2,367 kilojoules of heat: a coefficient of performance of about 24, which is 70 percent of the ideal Carnot limit. That's 29 kilowatt-hours per tonne of water, inside the 20-to-60 range vendors quote. And compression actually superheats the steam, to about 113 degrees; it doesn't make it wet.

COP = heat moved ÷ work in. Carnot COP = the best any heat pump could do between those two temperatures. A heat pump moving heat "uphill" only 10 K is very efficient, which is why COP is so high.

Slide 115:30 · 60 s

Lesson three surprised me: cheapest to run isn't always MVR. At Texas prices, about seven cents per kilowatt-hour for power and cheap natural gas, MVR costs $2.09 per tonne of water evaporated, but a five-effect evaporator costs $1.58. MVR only wins on energy below about 5.4 cents. But that steam price is fuel only; it leaves out the boiler and the extra evaporator bodies. So I added capital cost from a Department of Energy cost report, scaled to today's dollars. The total yearly cost bottoms out around a three-degree heater temperature difference: go smaller and the heater gets huge; go bigger and you pay for more compressor lift.

Multi-effect evaporator: a chain of evaporators where each one's vapor heats the next, so 1 kg of steam boils about N kg of water. CEPCI: an index used to scale old equipment prices to today. These costs are order-of-magnitude and labeled that way.

Slide 136:30 · 50 s

Lesson four: the purge is a dial between yield and purity. Sodium doesn't crystallize with the product, so it builds up in the liquid until the purge carries it out. With a five percent purge, sodium concentrates twenty times in the mother liquor, and about ten percent of the lithium leaves with the purge, so the yield is 89.6 percent. The crystals carry a thin film of that liquor, which is where the 583 parts-per-million impurity comes from. So there are two separate levers: purge for yield, washing for purity.

Mother liquor = the saturated liquid the crystals grow in. ε = kg of that liquid stuck to each kg of crystals after the centrifuge.

Slide 157:20 · 45 s

Lesson five is about AI: one model planned, another critiqued. Gemini returned ten points on Claude's plan, and Claude checked the numbers against steam tables before I decided. Nine accepted, four modified, one claim rejected. Gemini was right that compression superheats the vapor, and it caught an inconsistency in my purity model. But its one "critical" point was its own arithmetic slip. I kept its suggested fix and rejected the reasoning. The critic needs checking too.

Slide 178:05 · 45 s

Lesson six: two builds, same tests. Both models got the same plan, prompt and data, and both passed all nine hand-calculation tests on the first run. They took about the same time and cost, around thirteen minutes and three dollars each, but Gemini wrote 2.7 times the code, hid every equation behind buttons, and wrote that the course instructor approved its critique, which never happened. Glossary: terms in plain words

TermWhat it means (and the one-line "why")
MVRMechanical vapor recompression. A compressor squeezes the evaporator's own vapor so it condenses hotter and can heat the liquor again. It's a heat pump.
Latent heat (hfg)Energy to turn liquid water into vapor (~2,367 kJ/kg at 56 °C). Released again when the vapor condenses.
BPEBoiling-point elevation: dissolved ions make a solution boil hotter than pure water at the same pressure (3.95 K here).
Molality mmol of solute per kg of water (≈ 6.08 mol/kg for saturated LiOH at 60 °C).
Osmotic coefficient φCorrection for a real, concentrated solution (0.83): its ions act "less than ideal", so the real BPE is smaller than the ideal one.
LiftHow much the compressor raises the condensing temperature: BPE + heater ΔT ≈ 10 K.
Heater ΔTTemperature difference that drives heat across the heater tubes (6 K base). Smaller ΔT → bigger, costlier heater; bigger ΔT → more lift and power.
Pressure ratio rDischarge ÷ suction pressure (26.1 / 16.5 kPa = 1.58).
Isentropic efficiency ηisHow close the real compressor is to an ideal, loss-free one (0.75 assumed).
SuperheatCompressing steam heats it above its condensing temperature (to ~113 °C); it gets drier, not wetter.
COP / Carnot COPHeat moved per unit of work (≈ 24) / the theoretical best for the same temperatures (≈ 34).
kWh/tElectricity per tonne of water evaporated (29 base). The plant-comparison number; vendors quote 20–60.
Multi-effect (N effects)Evaporators in series, each heated by the previous one's vapor. Steam economy ≈ N kg water per kg steam, but each effect loses driving force to BPE.
Steam economy Ekg of water evaporated per kg of fresh steam.
Crossover price ce*The electricity price where MVR and an N-effect evaporator cost the same per tonne.
CEPCIChemical Engineering Plant Cost Index: scales 1998 equipment prices to 2023 dollars (×797.9/389.5).
TAC, CRFTotal annualized cost = capital spread over the equipment life (via the capital recovery factor) + yearly electricity.
TurndownRunning below design feed. With the heater already built, less heat needs a smaller ΔT, so less lift (chart P10).
Purge ratio pPurge flow ÷ feed flow (0.05). Sets how much sodium leaves, and how much lithium leaves with it.
Mother liquorThe saturated liquid the crystals grow in; impurities concentrate in it.
ε (adhering liquor)kg of mother liquor left on each kg of crystals after the centrifuge (0.03). Washing lowers it.
Yield / ppm% of fed lithium that ends up as crystals (89.6 %) / parts per million of impurity in the product (583).
SupersaturationConcentration above the solubility limit; the driving force for crystals to form and grow.
Metastable zoneJust above solubility: existing crystals grow, but new ones rarely form. Operating here (with seed crystals) gives fewer, larger crystals.
Binodal / spinodalBinodal = the solubility curve; spinodal = the limit where the solution becomes unstable to any disturbance. The site draws these schematically.
Antoine equationFit for water's vapor pressure vs temperature (checked within 0.38 % of NIST from 30–110 °C).

Likely questions (short answers)

Why does the compressor have to lift BPE? The vapor is pure water, so it condenses at water's saturation temperature, BPE below the liquor. To heat the liquor it must condense above the liquor's temperature.

Why is Kb 0.39, not 0.512? Kb = R·Tb²·Mw/ΔHvap. At 60 °C, T² is smaller than at 100 °C, so Kb is smaller.

Why doesn't compression make the steam wet? Compressing a vapor raises its temperature faster than its saturation temperature, so it leaves superheated (~113 °C vs 66 °C saturation). Liquid only comes from entrained mist or foam.

Why not cooling crystallization? LiOH solubility barely changes with temperature (13.4 vs 18.6 g/100 g water from 20 to 100 °C), so cooling recovers only a small fraction.

Why is steam "fuel-only"? It's the sourced fuel cost of making steam (EIA gas price). Boiler capital and labour aren't included, so it flatters multi-effect; I say so on the slide.

How did you know the AI was right? Tests were written from my hand calculation before either build; every data value has a source; Claude fact-checked Gemini's critique numbers against steam tables; I made every decision.

Why does a smaller heater ΔT save energy? Lift = BPE + ΔT. Less ΔT → less lift → less compressor work, but the heater area (A = Q/(U·ΔT)) grows.

What sets the product purity? The film of mother liquor on the crystals: impurity ≈ ε·zML/(1+ε), and zML = zF/p.
