Evaporative Crystallization of Battery-Grade $\text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}$

Why do modern lithium hydroxide refineries deploy Mechanical Vapor Recompression (MVR) heat pump evaporative crystallizers instead of traditional multi-effect steam trains?

The Industrial Problem

The global transition to electric mobility relies heavily on high-nickel lithium-ion battery chemistries (e.g., NMC 811 and NCA). Producing these cathodes requires battery-grade lithium hydroxide monohydrate ($\text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Chemical refining routes generate large volumes of aqueous lithium liquor. Because $\text{LiOH}$ exhibits an unusually flat aqueous solubility curve, cooling crystallization achieves poor yields, mandating evaporative crystallization. However, evaporating thousands of tonnes of water per day with fresh boiler steam requires massive thermal energy. MVR technology dramatically slashes this energy burden by upgrading low-pressure overhead vapor into heating steam with an electrically driven compressor.

Interactive Learning Objectives

  • Energy Thermodynamics: Discover how MVR acts as an open-loop heat pump, achieving effective Coefficients of Performance ($\text{COP} > 20$) by only paying for compressor lift ($\Delta T_{\text{lift}} = \text{BPE} + \Delta T_{\text{heater}}$) rather than latent heat of vaporization.
  • Electrolyte Colligative Limits: Analyze why Boiling Point Elevation ($\text{BPE}$) erodes the thermodynamic driving force of multi-effect evaporators in series while directly increasing MVR compression ratios.
  • Utility Economics: Identify the electricity vs. steam operating cost crossover points, benchmarking real-world industrial utility rates from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA).
  • Mass & Purge Balances: Balance lithium recovery yield against product purity by adjusting mother liquor purge ratios and crystal cake washing.

What I Learned & What I Want to Learn Next

What I learned: Vapor recompression achieves massive primary energy reductions by recirculating latent heat that would otherwise be discarded into cooling towers. Colligative boiling point elevation represents a pure parasitic thermodynamic penalty that directly elevates the compressor pressure ratio and discharge temperature. In multi-effect evaporators, BPE compounds across every stage, rapidly rendering high effect numbers infeasible.

What I want to learn next: In Phase 2, how to rigorously balance the capital expenditure of heat exchanger surface area against compressor electrical consumption (optimum $\Delta T_{\text{heater}}$), and how fixed-area crystallizers handle turndown during plant operational swings.

Industry Context: The Lithium Refining Revolution

1. Why Lithium Hydroxide for High-Nickel Cathodes?

Lithium-ion battery cathodes with high nickel content (such as NMC 811: 80% Ni, 10% Mn, 10% Co) require lithium hydroxide ($\text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}$) rather than lithium carbonate ($\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$). Nickel-rich cathode precursors cannot withstand the high calcination temperatures ($>900\ ^\circ\text{C}$) required to decompose lithium carbonate without inducing nickel reduction ($\text{Ni}^{3+} \rightarrow \text{Ni}^{2+}$) and destructive cation mixing in the crystal lattice. Lithium hydroxide melts and reacts at lower synthesis temperatures ($\sim 700\ ^\circ\text{C}$), ensuring optimal cathode crystallinity and performance.

3. Production Volume Arithmetic

What does a commercial battery-scale refinery look like in numbers?

For a battery production capacity of $50\ \text{GWh/yr}$:

$$\text{Battery Demand} = 50\ \text{GWh/yr} \times 0.7\ \frac{\text{kg LCE}}{\text{kWh}}\ (\text{ESTIMATE}) \approx 35,000\ \text{t LCE/yr}$$

Converting Lithium Carbonate Equivalent (LCE, $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$, $73.89\ \text{g/mol}$) to Lithium Hydroxide Monohydrate ($\text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $41.965\ \text{g/mol}$):

$$\text{Conversion Factor} = \frac{2 \times 41.965}{73.89} = 1.136\ \frac{\text{t}\ \text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}}{\text{t LCE}}$$ $$\text{Hydrate Product} = 35,000 \times 1.136 \approx 40,000\ \text{t}\ \text{LiOH}\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O/yr} \approx 5\ \text{t/h}$$

At a typical crystallizer feed concentration of $6.0\ \text{wt\%}\ \text{LiOH}$, this requires a continuous crystallizer feed rate of approximately $\mathbf{50\ \text{t/h}}$ ($50,000\ \text{kg/h}$) and evaporates over $42\ \text{t/h}$ of water!

4. Commercial Hardware: Forced-Circulation MVR Crystallizers

Industrial suppliers such as Veolia Water Technologies (HPD crystallization systems, US Patent 10,961,126 B2) utilize forced-circulation (FC) evaporative crystallizers. High circulation velocities prevent boiling inside the heat exchanger tubes, suppressing localized tube scaling and driving flash evaporation exclusively in the vapor disengagement chamber. Market studies estimate MVR commands roughly 55–60% of newly installed industrial evaporative crystallization capacity (Meticulous Research, market estimate, ESTIMATE).

5. Operational Problem Callouts

Problem 1: Severe Foaming & Liquid Carry-Over

Phenomenon: High vapor velocities combined with trace organic impurities, surfactants, or carbonate fines create stable foam heads in the vapor body.

Impact on MVR: Liquid droplet carry-over into the high-speed compressor suction causes severe mechanical droplet erosion on titanium/hastelloy impellers, dynamic unbalance, and alkaline contamination of the condensed vapor.

Mitigation: Conservative vapor body sizing ($v_{\text{vapor}} < 1.5\ \text{m/s}$), dual-stage wire-mesh demisters with wash sprays, and continuous addition of food-grade silicone antifoam emulsions.

Problem 2: $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ Inverse-Solubility Scaling

Phenomenon: Contact with ambient air or residual process carbonate leads to carbonate formation. Unlike $\text{LiOH}$, lithium carbonate ($\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$) exhibits inverse (retrograde) solubility—it becomes less soluble at higher temperatures.

Impact on MVR: As circulating liquor contacts the hot heat exchanger tube walls, $\text{Li}_2\text{CO}_3$ precipitates directly onto the tube surface, creating a hard, insulating mineral scale that rapidly degrades the overall heat transfer coefficient ($U$).

Mitigation: Strict upstream ion-exchange decarbonation, pressurized liquid circulation to ensure zero boiling in tubes, and automated periodic chemical cleaning (clean-in-place, CIP) cycles.

Separation Process Theory & Governing Equations

Every governing relationship from chemical engineering separations is detailed below with its formula, dimensional units, and physical interpretation.

1. Mass Balances & Recovery

Equation M1: Total Mass Balance [kg/h]
$$F = V + C + P$$
Physical Interpretation: Everything fed leaves as vapor, crystals, or purge.
Equation M2: Lithium Mass Balance [kg LiOH/h]
$$F x_F = 0.5707\, C + P x_L$$
Physical Interpretation: Lithium leaves only in crystals or purge, so a bigger purge means less product.
Equation M3: Impurity Solute Balance [kg/h]
$$F z_F = P z_{\text{ML}}$$
Physical Interpretation: Impurities don't crystallize, so the purge is their only exit.
Equation M4: Mother Liquor Impurity Concentration [dimensionless]
$$z_{\text{ML}} = \frac{z_F}{p}$$
Physical Interpretation: The mother liquor concentrates impurity by $1/p$.
Equation M5: Saturated Liquor Mass Fraction [mass fraction]
$$x_L = (1 - z_{\text{ML}}) \frac{s}{100 + s}$$
Physical Interpretation: Saturated liquor: the LiOH:water ratio is fixed by solubility at $T$.
Equation M6: Water Mass Balance [kg/h]
$$F(1 - x_F - z_F) = V + 0.4293\, C + P(1 - x_L - z_{\text{ML}})$$
Physical Interpretation: Water leaves as vapor, as hydrate water, or in the purge.
Equation M7: Lithium Recovery Yield [fraction]
$$\text{Yield} = \frac{0.5707\, C}{F x_F}$$
Physical Interpretation: Fraction of the incoming Li recovered.
Equation M8: Product Crystal Impurity [ppm]
$$\text{Impurity} \approx \frac{\varepsilon z_{\text{ML}}}{1 + \varepsilon} \times 10^6$$
Physical Interpretation: Purity-only model: the liquor film left on the crystals after the centrifuge carries impurity. Its mass is neglected in M1–M6.
Equation M9: Theoretical Cooling Crystallization Yield [fraction]
$$\text{Cooling Yield} = \frac{s(T_{\text{hot}}) - s(T_{\text{cold}})}{s(T_{\text{hot}})}$$
Physical Interpretation: Flat solubility: cooling recovers little, so water must be evaporated.

2. Phase Equilibria, BPE & Crystallization Kinetics

Equation B1: Boiling Point Elevation (BPE) [K]
$$\text{BPE} = a \cdot i \cdot K_b \cdot m, \quad K_b = \frac{R\, T_b^2\, M_w}{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}$$
Physical Interpretation: Dissolved ions lower water's vapor pressure, so the liquor must be hotter to boil at the same pressure. $K_b$ is taken at $T_b$ for simplicity.
Equation B2: Boiling Liquor Temperature [°C]
$$T_b = T_{\text{sat}}(P) + \text{BPE}$$
Physical Interpretation: Non-volatile LiOH stays in the liquid, so the boiling liquor is at $T_b$. It makes pure water vapor at pressure $P$, superheated by BPE above that vapor's condensation point $T_{\text{sat}}(P)$.
Equation B3: Antoine Vapor Pressure [mmHg, kPa]
$$\log_{10} P_{\text{sat}} [\text{mmHg}] = A - \frac{B}{C + T\,[^\circ\text{C}]}$$
Physical Interpretation: Higher $T \rightarrow$ higher vapor pressure. The compressor raises $P$ so the vapor condenses hotter.
Equation B4: Supersaturation Ratio & Driving Force [dimensionless]
$$S = \frac{c}{c^*}, \quad \sigma = \frac{c - c^*}{c^*}$$
Physical Interpretation: Driving force for crystallization: how far above saturation the liquor is.
Equation B5: Phase Boundaries (Binodal & Spinodal) [concentration]
$$\text{Binodal } (c^*) < \text{Metastable Limit} < \text{Spinodal Limit}$$
Physical Interpretation: Below $c^*$: crystals dissolve. Metastable zone: existing seed crystals grow, but new nuclei rarely form, so seeding controls size. Above the metastable limit: nucleation shower $\rightarrow$ fines. Spinodal: thermodynamic limit where phase separation needs no nucleation barrier.

Why Crystal Size Distribution Matters

Crystals that are too small (fines): Slower centrifuge dewatering rates, higher adhering mother liquor retention ($\varepsilon$), resulting in excessive impurity carryover (elevated ppm Na) and severe airborne dust hazards during pneumatic conveyance and dry packaging.

Crystals that are too large: Excessive agglomeration traps mother liquor inclusions inside the crystal matrix (which cannot be washed away by surface cake rinsing), longer required residence times, and crystal attrition/breakage in pumps.

3. Energy & Multi-Effect Steam Evaporation

Equation E1: Evaporator Heat Duty [kW]
$$Q \approx \frac{V\, h_{fg}(T_v)}{3600}$$
Physical Interpretation: Almost all the energy goes into boiling water off.
Equation E2: Steam Consumption & Economy [kg/h, dimensionless]
$$S = \frac{Q}{h_{fg}(T_s)}, \quad E = \frac{V}{S}$$
Physical Interpretation: One kg of steam boils about one kg of water.
Equation E3: Multi-Effect Economy [dimensionless]
$$E_N \approx N \cdot E_1$$
Physical Interpretation: Each effect's vapor heats the next, so the latent heat is reused $N$ times (an upper bound).
Equation E4: Multi-Effect Temperature Penalty [K]
$$\Delta T_{\text{per effect}} = \frac{T_s - T_{\text{last}} - N \cdot \text{BPE}}{N} \ge \Delta T_{\text{min}}$$
Physical Interpretation: In each effect, vapor leaves the liquor at $T_{b,k}$ but condenses in the next heater only at $T_{b,k} - \text{BPE}$, so BPE is subtracted from the usable driving force in every stage.

4. Mechanical Vapor Recompression (MVR) Heat Pump

Equation C1: Saturation Pressures & Temperatures [°C, kPa]
$$T_c = T_b + \Delta T_{\text{heater}}, \quad P_2 = P_{\text{sat}}(T_c), \quad P_1 = P_{\text{sat}}(T_b - \text{BPE})$$
Physical Interpretation: The vapor must be compressed until it condenses hotter than the liquor it heats.
Equation C2: Pressure Ratio [dimensionless]
$$r = \frac{P_2}{P_1}$$
Physical Interpretation: Set by the required lift ($\text{BPE} + \Delta T_{\text{heater}}$).
Equation C3: Isentropic Compression Work [kJ/kg]
$$w_s = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1} R_v T_1 \left(r^{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}} - 1\right) / 1000$$
Physical Interpretation: Work grows with the lift, not with the latent heat recycled. It scales with $T_1$ and $R/M$ because hotter, lighter vapor has a larger specific volume ($v = RT/(PM)$), so more $\int v\, dP$ work is needed per kg for the same pressure ratio.
Equation C4: Real Work & Electrical Power [kJ/kg, kW]
$$w = \frac{w_s}{\eta_{\text{is}}}, \quad W_{\text{elec}} = \frac{V \cdot w}{3600 \cdot \eta_{\text{motor}}}$$
Physical Interpretation: Real machines and motors lose some work as heat.
Equation C5: Specific Energy Consumption [kWh/t vapor]
$$\text{kWh/t} = \frac{w}{3.6 \cdot \eta_{\text{motor}}}$$
Physical Interpretation: The plant-comparison number (vendor range 20–60, ESTIMATE).
Equation C6: Coefficient of Performance (COP) [dimensionless]
$$\text{COP} = \frac{h_{fg}}{w}, \quad \text{COP}_{\text{Carnot}} = \frac{T_c + 273.15}{T_c - T_v}$$
Physical Interpretation: A heat pump: ~22 units of heat moved per unit of work, because only the lift is paid for.
Equation C7: Compressor Discharge Superheat [°C]
$$T_2 = T_1 \left[1 + \frac{r^{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}} - 1}{\eta_{\text{is}}}\right] - 273.15$$
Physical Interpretation: Compression superheats steam (≈113–117 °C out vs 66 °C condensing); it doesn't make it wet. Liquid reaches the compressor only as entrained mist or foam (erosion, LiOH carry-over), or deliberately as condensate spray to desuperheat.
Equation C8: Compressor Surplus Heat [kJ/kg]
$$q_{\text{delivered}} \approx h_{fg}(T_v) + c_{p,v}\,\text{BPE} + w > h_{fg}(T_c)$$
Physical Interpretation: The compressor work doesn't disappear: it shows up as ~3 % surplus heat, used for feed preheat and losses, or vented.

5. Operating Economics

Equation $1: MVR Operating Cost [$/t vapor]
$$\text{cost}_{\text{MVR}} = c_e \cdot \text{kWh/t}$$
Physical Interpretation: You pay only for the lift.
Equation $2: Multi-Effect Operating Cost [$/t vapor]
$$\text{cost}_N = \frac{c_s}{E_N}$$
Physical Interpretation: You pay for steam, divided by the number of reuses.
Equation $3: Economic Utility Crossover Price [$/kWh]
$$c_e^* = \frac{c_s}{E_N \cdot \text{kWh/t}}$$
Physical Interpretation: Below this electricity price, MVR is cheaper to run.
Equation $4: Annual Operating Cost [$/yr]
$$\text{Annual Cost} = \text{cost/t} \times V \times \frac{\text{hours}}{1000}$$
Physical Interpretation: Scales to the plant.
